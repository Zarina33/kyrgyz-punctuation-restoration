%% ABSTRACT
%% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
We present the first punctuation restoration system for Kyrgyz, a low-resource agglutinative language of the Turkic family with approximately 7~million speakers. Punctuation restoration is a critical post-processing step for automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems, yet no prior work has addressed this task for Kyrgyz. We construct a labeled dataset of 16{,}028 sentences drawn from literary texts, Wikipedia, and news sources, including a targeted question-mark expansion that raises the \texttt{QUESTION} token count from 940 to 2{,}938. We compare three transformer encoders fine-tuned for token-level classification over the labels \texttt{O}, \texttt{PERIOD}, \texttt{COMMA}, and \texttt{QUESTION}: two multilingual models (mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa-base) and the Kyrgyz-specific monolingual KyrgyzBERT. XLM-RoBERTa achieves the best overall weighted F1-score of 94.4\%, outperforming mBERT (93.0\%), KyrgyzBERT (92.1\%), and a rule-based baseline (76.8\%). We additionally (i)~enumerate the full punctuation inventory of written Kyrgyz and justify the scope of marks covered by our system, (ii)~benchmark the inference efficiency of the three transformer models, and (iii)~evaluate an ASR-integration pipeline on Common~Voice Kyrgyz in which our post-processor adds 589~commas and raises sentence-terminal coverage from 88\% to 100\% on 200~utterances produced by a Kyrgyz-fine-tuned Whisper model. We release our dataset and all three fine-tuned models publicly to support further research on Turkic language processing.\footnote{Code: \url{https://github.com/Zarina33/kyrgyz-punctuation-restoration}. Dataset: \url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/Zarinaaa/kyrgyz-punctuation-dataset}. Models: \url{https://huggingface.co/Zarinaaa/xlmr-kyrgyz-punctuation}, \url{https://huggingface.co/Zarinaaa/mbert-kyrgyz-punctuation}, \url{https://huggingface.co/Zarinaaa/kyrgyzbert-kyrgyz-punctuation}.}
\end{abstract}

%% 1. INTRODUCTION
%% ============================================================
\section{Introduction}

Punctuation restoration, the task of inserting appropriate punctuation marks into unpunctuated text, is an essential post-processing step for automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems. ASR output typically lacks punctuation, which degrades readability for human readers and reduces the effectiveness of downstream NLP tasks such as machine translation, summarization, and named-entity recognition~\cite{nguyen2019fast, yi2019self}.

Early approaches to punctuation restoration relied on hand-crafted rules and n-gram language models. The introduction of recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks brought substantial improvements, enabling models to capture longer-range dependencies in text~\cite{li2020multilingual}. The transformer architecture~\cite{vaswani2017attention} and pre-trained language models such as BERT~\cite{devlin2019bert} and RoBERTa~\cite{liu2019roberta} further advanced the state of the art, with XLM-RoBERTa~\cite{conneau2020unsupervised} extending these capabilities to over 100~languages through cross-lingual pre-training on 2.5~TB of filtered CommonCrawl data.

Despite these advances, punctuation-restoration research has focused predominantly on high-resource languages such as English, Chinese, and European languages~\cite{alam2020punctuation, guerreiro2021subtitles, nagy2021punctuation}, leaving low-resource languages largely unstudied. This gap is particularly problematic for languages such as Kyrgyz, where ASR systems are beginning to emerge but lack the post-processing pipeline needed for practical deployment.

Kyrgyz presents specific challenges for punctuation restoration. As an agglutinative Turkic language with SOV (Subject-Object-Verb) word order, Kyrgyz exhibits complex morphological patterns in which a single word may carry multiple suffixes encoding grammatical relations. Converb suffixes (e.g., \textit{-p/-yp/-ip}) frequently mark clause boundaries where commas should appear, and the verb-final structure means that sentence-ending punctuation depends on recognizing finite verb forms. These morphosyntactic properties are not shared by the high-resource languages on which most punctuation restoration systems are evaluated.

In this paper, we make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We present the \textbf{first punctuation restoration system} for the Kyrgyz language, demonstrating that multilingual transfer learning is effective for agglutinative low-resource languages.
    \item We construct and release a \textbf{labeled Kyrgyz dataset} of 16{,}028 sentences for punctuation restoration, including a targeted expansion that triples the \texttt{QUESTION}-token count to address pronounced class imbalance on interrogatives.
    \item We provide a \textbf{three-way comparative evaluation} of two multilingual transformer models (mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa) and the Kyrgyz-specific monolingual model KyrgyzBERT, plus a rule-based baseline, with detailed per-class analysis.
    \item We document the \textbf{complete punctuation inventory} of written Kyrgyz and explicitly justify the scope of marks our system handles (Section~\ref{sec:punct-inventory}).
    \item We benchmark the \textbf{inference efficiency} of the three transformer models (Section~\ref{sec:inference-efficiency}) and evaluate an \textbf{ASR-integration pipeline} on Common~Voice Kyrgyz (Section~\ref{sec:asr-integration}), showing that even a Kyrgyz-fine-tuned Whisper fails to insert commas and that our post-processor is the component that yields a reader-usable transcript.
    \item We present a \textbf{linguistically informed error analysis} that connects model errors to specific morphosyntactic properties of Kyrgyz.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Kyrgyz Language Background}
\label{sec:kyrgyz}

Kyrgyz (ISO 639-3: \texttt{kir}) is a Turkic language of the Kipchak branch, spoken by approximately 7~million people, primarily in the Kyrgyz Republic. It is written in Cyrillic script with 36~letters, including three letters absent from the Russian Cyrillic alphabet: \cyr{Ө}, \cyr{Ү}, and \cyr{Ң}. A recent survey of Kyrgyz NLP resources~\cite{alekseev2024kyrgyznlp} characterizes Kyrgyz as ``severely under-resourced,'' highlighting the scarcity of annotated datasets and language-specific models. Understanding the linguistic properties of Kyrgyz is essential for interpreting our model's behavior and error patterns.

\subsection{Morphological Properties}

Kyrgyz is a highly agglutinative language in which words are formed by concatenating multiple suffixes to a root. A single word can encode information that would require an entire phrase in English. For example:

\begin{quote}
\textit{okutuchularybyzdyng} = \textit{oku} (read) + \textit{-tuuchu} (agent) + \textit{-lar} (plural) + \textit{-ybyz} (1st person plural possessive) + \textit{-dyng} (genitive) = `of our teachers'
\end{quote}

This agglutinative structure has direct consequences for punctuation restoration. First, subword tokenization algorithms such as BPE (used by XLM-RoBERTa) fragment Kyrgyz words into multiple tokens, meaning the model must learn to associate punctuation decisions with token sequences rather than individual words. Second, certain morphological markers are strong indicators of punctuation placement: converb suffixes (\textit{-p, -yp, -ip}) typically signal comma positions, as they mark clause boundaries in complex sentences.

\subsection{Syntactic Properties}

Kyrgyz follows SOV (Subject-Object-Verb) word order, with the finite verb appearing at the end of the sentence. Period placement therefore depends on recognizing sentence-final verb forms, a signal very different from that of SVO languages such as English, where sentence boundaries are marked by subject-initial patterns.

Complex sentences in Kyrgyz are constructed using participial and converbal forms rather than relative pronouns. Subordinate clauses are typically formed by suffixing the verb, which creates long, multi-clause sentences in which comma placement depends on morphological analysis rather than on syntactic keywords.

\subsection{Punctuation Inventory of Written Kyrgyz}
\label{sec:punct-inventory}

Written Kyrgyz shares the punctuation system of modern Russian\footnote{Both languages use Cyrillic script and have been in contact for over a century; Kyrgyz orthographic and punctuation norms were standardized during the Soviet period largely following Russian conventions.} with two minor adaptations: the dash~(\textendash) is used extensively as a copula substitute (Kyrgyz lacks an overt ``to be'' in the present tense), and the vocative comma appears in direct-address constructions. \paragraph{Scope of this work.} We model the three punctuation marks (\texttt{.}, \texttt{,}, \texttt{?}) that (i)~carry the highest information load for readability of ASR output and (ii)~are unambiguously local: their placement is determined by the token they follow. These three marks alone cover 100\% of sentence-final positions and 98.6\% of all non-paired punctuation tokens in the corpus.

We explicitly \emph{exclude} the remaining marks for the following reasons. The \textbf{exclamation mark} (\texttt{!}) almost never appears as the absolute sentence terminator in written Kyrgyz; it is embedded inside direct-speech segments that themselves end with a period following the closing quote or dash, and treating it as a token-level label would require additional direct-speech modeling. The \textbf{dash} (\texttt{\textendash}) as a copula substitute has a placement that depends on the underlying syntactic parse (``\textit{Мен \textendash{} студентмин}'' = ``I am a student''), not on morphological cues, and accurate dash restoration would require a dependency parser, which itself does not exist for Kyrgyz at the time of writing. \textbf{Quotation marks} (\texttt{«\,»}, \texttt{"\,"}) and \textbf{parentheses} (\texttt{(\,)}) are \emph{paired} marks; token-level classification cannot guarantee bracket balance without a structured decoder. Finally, the \textbf{colon} (\texttt{:}), \textbf{semicolon} (\texttt{;}), \textbf{em-dash} (\texttt{\textemdash}), and \textbf{ellipsis} (\texttt{\ldots}) are each below 1\% of all punctuation tokens; training a reliable classifier for them would require either focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal} or targeted data collection beyond the scope of this paper. We revisit these exclusions in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Transformer-based punctuation restoration.}
Alam et al.~\cite{alam2020punctuation} fine-tuned XLM-RoBERTa for punctuation restoration on English and Bangla, achieving F1-scores of 87.0\% on clean English text and 69.5\% on Bangla. Their work demonstrated that multilingual pre-trained models can transfer to low-resource languages, though with a significant performance gap. Yi and Tao~\cite{yi2019self} combined self-attention with acoustic embeddings for English ASR transcripts. Guerreiro et al.~\cite{guerreiro2021subtitles} evaluated multiple transformer models across European languages, confirming that XLM-RoBERTa achieves strong cross-lingual performance even with minimal target-language data.

\textbf{Multilingual and low-resource approaches.}
Li and Lin~\cite{li2020multilingual} trained a multilingual LSTM model with BPE encoding across 43~languages, showing that shared multilingual representations benefit low-resource targets. Chordia~\cite{chordia2021punktuator} compared mBERT and XLM-RoBERTa across multiple languages including Hindi and Tamil, finding that XLM-RoBERTa consistently outperformed mBERT for morphologically complex languages. Pan et al.~\cite{pan2022punctuation} applied pre-trained models to multilingual ASR outputs, addressing domain mismatch between written training data and spoken language.

\textbf{Turkic languages.}
Kurt and \c{C}ay{\i}r~\cite{kurt2023turkish} developed transformer-based models for Turkish punctuation restoration, reporting macro F1-scores up to 83.9\%. More recently, G\"{o}rmez et al.~\cite{gormez2025turkish} constructed a new Turkish dataset and trained CNN and transformer-encoder models, achieving an overall F-score of 90.1\%. Both studies confirm the challenges of comma prediction in agglutinative Turkic languages. Kyrgyz shares agglutinative morphology and SOV word order with Turkish, so these works serve as natural reference points, despite the two languages belonging to different Turkic branches (Oghuz and Kipchak).

\textbf{Streaming and real-time approaches.}
Pol\'{a}\v{c}ek et al.~\cite{polacek2023streaming} proposed a lightweight ELECTRA-based online punctuation restoration module for streaming ASR, achieving F1 of 71.2\% on Czech radio transcripts with a latency of only three words. Their work highlights the practical constraints of real-time deployment, a direction relevant for future Kyrgyz ASR systems.

\textbf{Data augmentation for low-resource NLP.}
Wei and Zou~\cite{wei2019eda} proposed Easy Data Augmentation (EDA) techniques that improve text classification performance in low-resource settings. Fu et al.~\cite{fu2021improving} showed that external data selection using n-gram language models can improve punctuation restoration for speech transcripts by 1.12\% F1.

\textbf{Recent Kyrgyz NLP resources.}
Concurrent with our work, three recent resources have substantially improved the Kyrgyz NLP landscape. KyrgyzBERT~\cite{metinov2025kyrgyzbert} is a compact monolingual BERT pre-trained exclusively on Kyrgyz; we evaluate it as a baseline (Sections~\ref{sec:main-results} and~\ref{sec:inference-efficiency}). KyrText~\cite{chubakov2026kyrtext} is a multi-domain 680.5M-word Kyrgyz corpus that supports continued pre-training of mBERT, XLM-R, and from-scratch RoBERTa training; a re-run of our experiments on top of these stronger Kyrgyz-adapted checkpoints is an obvious next step. Metinov~\cite{metinov2026russiankyrgyz} provides strong Russian$\leftrightarrow$Kyrgyz MT baselines that may be useful for cross-lingual transfer experiments in future work. For Hebrew punctuation, the \texttt{verbit/hebrew\_punctuation} Hugging Face model card provides an AlephBERT-based fine-tuned system without accompanying publication; it serves as the real-world deployment analogue that motivates our Kyrgyz-specific pipeline.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task Definition}

We formulate punctuation restoration as a token-level classification problem. Given a sequence of input tokens $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$, the model predicts a label $y_i \in \{$\texttt{O}, \texttt{PERIOD}, \texttt{COMMA}, \texttt{QUESTION}$\}$ for each token $x_i$, where the label indicates the punctuation mark that should follow the token (or \texttt{O} for no punctuation):

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i = \arg\max \; \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W} \cdot \mathbf{h}_i + \mathbf{b})
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathbf{h}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ is the hidden representation of token $x_i$ from the final transformer layer, $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 768}$ is a learnable weight matrix, and $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{4}$ is a bias vector.

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

No punctuation restoration dataset existed for Kyrgyz prior to this work. We constructed a dataset from three sources:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Literary texts} (67.5\%): Books in various genres (literature, science, history) downloaded from the Kyrgyz-Turkish Manas University library and converted from PDF to text.
    \item \textbf{Wikipedia} (20.0\%): Articles from the Kyrgyz-language Wikipedia.
    \item \textbf{News articles} (12.5\%): Texts from Kyrgyz-language news portals.
\end{itemize}

Text preprocessing proceeds in four stages. (i)~\textbf{Sentence segmentation.} Raw text is split into sentences using a regular-expression pattern that matches sentence-terminal punctuation (\texttt{.}, \texttt{?}, \texttt{!}) followed by whitespace and an upper-case letter. We rely on a regex rather than a general-purpose sentence splitter (e.g.\ NLTK \texttt{punkt} or spaCy) because Kyrgyz is not supported by those libraries, and the regex-based splitter is the standard approach for Cyrillic low-resource languages lacking dedicated segmenters. (ii)~\textbf{Word tokenization.} Each sentence is split on whitespace into whitespace-delimited tokens; subword tokenization is performed later inside the model's tokenizer. (iii)~\textbf{Label assignment.} Each token is assigned a label based on the punctuation mark immediately following it (\texttt{.}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{PERIOD}, \texttt{,}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{COMMA}, \texttt{?}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{QUESTION}, otherwise \texttt{O}); the trailing punctuation character is then stripped from the token. (iv)~\textbf{Filtering.} We removed duplicate sentences, corrected encoding errors specific to Cyrillic text, and discarded sentences containing fewer than 2~tokens. Input sequences longer than 256~subword tokens were truncated during the model's own tokenizer step. The full preprocessing code is released with the dataset.

We split the dataset into training (76.5\%), validation (8.5\%), and test (15\%) sets using random sampling with a fixed seed (\texttt{random\_state=42}) for reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Question-mark Augmentation}
\label{sec:augmentation}

The initial 14{,}028-sentence dataset contains only 940 \texttt{QUESTION} tokens across all splits. After the standard 76.5/8.5/15 train/val/test partition, the test set retains only 125~questions, which is insufficient for reliable class-wise evaluation. We therefore expand the training data with 2{,}000 additional question-ending sentences drawn from an independent in-house cleaned Kyrgyz text corpus (a 1.6M-line UTF-8 plain-text collection of Kyrgyz literary, news, and Wikipedia material curated by the authors during prior data-collection work, with the same domain mix as the primary dataset) and deduplicated string-wise against the original dataset. Candidate sentences are required to (i)~end with \texttt{?}, (ii)~contain 3--30 words, (iii)~consist of a single sentence, and (iv)~contain Cyrillic characters. Sampling uses seed~42 for reproducibility, and the resulting \texttt{question\_data.json} file is released alongside the augmented training set on the dataset Hugging Face repository.

The expansion raises the \texttt{QUESTION} token count from 940 to 2{,}938 (a $3.1\times$ increase) and the \texttt{QUESTION} test-set support from 125 to 470, while leaving the distribution of the other three classes largely unchanged. All experiments reported in Sections~\ref{sec:results} onwards are conducted on this augmented dataset with an identical 76.5/8.5/15 split. The code that produces the expansion is released alongside the models.

\subsection{Models}

We compare three pre-trained transformer models, all fine-tuned on our augmented Kyrgyz dataset with a linear classification head appended on top of the final hidden states for token-level prediction.

\textbf{mBERT} (bert-base-multilingual-cased~\cite{devlin2019bert}) is pre-trained on Wikipedia text in 104~languages using masked language modeling and next-sentence prediction. It uses WordPiece tokenization and contains approximately 177\,M parameters (12~layers, 12~attention heads, 768-dimensional hidden states).

\textbf{XLM-RoBERTa-base}~\cite{conneau2020unsupervised} is pre-trained on 2.5~TB of filtered CommonCrawl data in 100~languages including Kyrgyz. It uses SentencePiece BPE tokenization and contains approximately 277\,M parameters (12~layers, 12~attention heads, 768-dimensional hidden states). Unlike mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa was trained with larger batches and more data, and does not use next-sentence prediction.

\textbf{KyrgyzBERT}~\cite{metinov2025kyrgyzbert} is a recent Kyrgyz-specific monolingual model (HuggingFace ID \texttt{metinovadilet/KyrgyzBert}). It is a compact BERT with 6~transformer layers, 8~attention heads, and 512-dimensional hidden states (approximately 35.6\,M parameters); it uses WordPiece tokenization and was pre-trained exclusively on Kyrgyz text. A plausible intuition is that a model pre-trained on the target language should outperform multilingual alternatives on downstream Kyrgyz tasks; we test this hypothesis directly.

For reference, we also evaluate a \textbf{rule-based baseline}: a deterministic system that inserts a period at sentence boundaries and a comma every $M$~tokens, where $M$ is set to the mean clause length in the training data ($M=5$). The baseline does not predict question marks, assigning all sentence-final positions the \texttt{PERIOD} label. All training and evaluation was performed using the HuggingFace Transformers library~\cite{wolf2019huggingface}.

\subsection{Label Assignment}

All three tokenizers split Kyrgyz words into multiple subword tokens, so each word's punctuation label must be assigned to exactly one subtoken. We assign the label to the \textbf{last subtoken} of each word; all other subtokens receive the special ignore index $-100$ and are excluded from the loss computation. This strategy forces the model to accumulate contextual information across the full subword sequence before making a prediction, which is particularly relevant for agglutinative Kyrgyz words where the punctuation-relevant morphological suffixes occur at the word end.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate using precision (P), recall (R), and F1-score at the token level, computed both per-class and as a weighted average. We also report the full confusion matrix to analyze error patterns across punctuation classes.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main Results}

\paragraph{Zero-shot KyrgyzBERT (no fine-tuning).}
To quantify the contribution of task-specific fine-tuning, we additionally evaluated KyrgyzBERT in a \emph{zero-shot} setting. We use the pre-trained masked-LM checkpoint as-is, with no exposure to our punctuation labels. For each test sentence we insert a \texttt{[MASK]} token after every word and, in a single forward pass, read off the softmax distribution at each mask; for every gap we compare the probabilities of `.', `,', and `?' to the aggregate probability of all other (non-punctuation) tokens, and predict the argmax. The resulting weighted F1 is 0.769 (vs.~0.921 for the fine-tuned checkpoint), with a striking per-class breakdown: \texttt{O}~F1=0.904, \texttt{COMMA}~F1=0.505, \texttt{PERIOD}~F1=0.001, \texttt{QUESTION}~F1=0.000. The zero-shot model never predicts a sentence-terminal mark: across the 1{,}967 true \texttt{PERIOD} tokens in the test set it assigns \texttt{PERIOD} to exactly one, and it never predicts \texttt{QUESTION}. The cause is distributional: commas are the most frequent punctuation token in the pre-training corpus and therefore dominate the mid-sentence \texttt{[MASK]} distribution; without task supervision, the MLM has no way of knowing that the current \texttt{[MASK]} is in a sentence-terminal position. This is a strong empirical confirmation that fine-tuning on labeled punctuation data is necessary even for a Kyrgyz-specific MLM; pre-training distribution alone is not enough.

\subsection{ASR Integration}
\label{sec:asr-integration}

\paragraph{Whisper-large-v3 does not support Kyrgyz.} OpenAI's multilingual Whisper model~\cite{radford2023whisper} exposes a fixed inventory of 100~language tokens through its tokenizer\footnote{\url{https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/tokenizer.py}, mirrored in the HuggingFace Transformers library as \texttt{transformers.models.whisper.tokenization\_whisper.LANGUAGES}.}. \section{Error Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Comma Prediction Challenges}

Comma prediction remains the most challenging class for all three models, with XLM-RoBERTa achieving F1~0.767, mBERT 0.698, and KyrgyzBERT 0.660. The difficulty is linked to Kyrgyz converb constructions. Converbal clauses (marked by suffixes such as \textit{-p, -yp, -ip}) function as subordinate clauses and are typically separated by commas. However, the models frequently fail to recognize these morphological cues as comma indicators, particularly in shorter clauses where the converb suffix is the only signal. For example, in \textit{``mushtumdaryn t\"{u}y\"{u}p, kayra achty''} (`clenching his fists, he opened [them] again'), the converb suffix \textit{-p} signals a comma, but the model may not identify it as a clause boundary in the absence of other syntactic cues. XLM-RoBERTa's advantage over mBERT (+0.069 F1) and over KyrgyzBERT (+0.107 F1) suggests that its larger and more diverse pre-training corpus better captures the distributional patterns around Kyrgyz clause boundaries. The ASR-integration study in Section~\ref{sec:asr-integration} further exposes the complementary weakness: the model is trained on literary text and therefore tends to \emph{over-insert} commas on short, factual utterances from Common~Voice, where converb-based subordinate clauses are rare.

\subsection{Question Mark Detection and the Effect of Augmentation}

In the initial 14{,}028-sentence dataset, the \texttt{QUESTION} class was sharply under-represented: only 940~tokens in total, 125 in the test set, and F1-scores of 0.704~(XLM-R), 0.643~(mBERT), and 0.410~(KyrgyzBERT). We therefore expanded the training data with 2{,}000 additional question-bearing sentences (Section~\ref{sec:augmentation}), tripling the \texttt{QUESTION} token count and quadrupling its test-set support (125~$\to$~470). Kyrgyz forms questions through suffixal particles (\textit{-by/-py}) and intonation rather than word-order inversion, which makes question detection a morphological signal-detection task that responds well to additional examples. Focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal} and class-weighted training are complementary techniques that may further improve question detection in future work.

\subsection{Subword Tokenization Effects}

All three models fragment agglutinative Kyrgyz words into multiple subword tokens, but with different tokenization algorithms and training data: mBERT uses WordPiece trained primarily on Wikipedia; XLM-RoBERTa uses SentencePiece BPE trained on a larger and more diverse CommonCrawl corpus that includes Kyrgyz text; and KyrgyzBERT uses WordPiece trained exclusively on Kyrgyz. In every case, the punctuation label is attached to the last subword of each word, so the model must propagate information across subword boundaries. This is particularly challenging for long Kyrgyz words with multiple suffixes, where the punctuation-relevant morphological information (for example, a converb marker) may be several subword positions away from the token that carries the punctuation label. XLM-RoBERTa's consistent advantage across classes suggests that its tokenizer better preserves the morphological structure of Kyrgyz words; KyrgyzBERT, despite a purely Kyrgyz vocabulary, is limited by its smaller pre-training corpus and shallower encoder.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This study has several limitations that scope its conclusions and point to concrete avenues for future work.

\paragraph{Domain.} Our dataset consists primarily of formal written text from literary works, Wikipedia, and news portals. Performance on conversational Kyrgyz or on truly spontaneous ASR-generated text may differ substantially. The ASR-integration experiment in Section~\ref{sec:asr-integration} partly addresses this gap by evaluating on Common~Voice audio, but a large-scale conversational benchmark for Kyrgyz does not yet exist.

\paragraph{Punctuation coverage.} As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:punct-inventory}, we model three punctuation marks (\texttt{.}, \texttt{,}, \texttt{?}) that together account for 98.6\% of non-paired punctuation tokens in our corpus. Paired marks (quotation marks, parentheses), the dash~(\textendash) used as a copula substitute, and low-frequency marks (\texttt{:}, \texttt{;}, \texttt{!}, \texttt{\ldots}) are explicitly excluded for the reasons given there. Extending coverage to these would require, respectively, structured decoders for paired marks, a Kyrgyz dependency parser for dash placement, and targeted data collection for the rare classes.

\paragraph{Data-augmentation scope.} The question-mark expansion described in Section~\ref{sec:augmentation} sources additional sentences from the same corpus family (literary and news Kyrgyz). It therefore increases support for the \texttt{QUESTION} class without broadening the genre distribution. A natural extension would be to draw interrogative sentences from conversational sources (e.g.\ subtitle or dialogue corpora) once these become available.

\paragraph{Domain transfer to ASR output.} The ASR integration study (Section~\ref{sec:asr-integration}) shows that our model, trained on literary text, can \emph{over-insert} commas on the shorter and more factual utterances of Common~Voice. On this out-of-domain input the model's literary-trained comma prior does not match the local discourse structure. A deployment pipeline would benefit either from a domain-adapted comma classifier or from a simple confidence-threshold mechanism at inference; both are left for future work.

\paragraph{ASR integration evaluation.} Common~Voice transcripts are normalized (lowercased, no punctuation), so the ASR output cannot be compared to a ground-truth punctuated reference. We therefore report only surface punctuation statistics (\% of utterances with a given mark). A Kyrgyz audio corpus with ground-truth punctuation would enable direct end-to-end punctuation-F1 evaluation of the ASR+post-processor pipeline; constructing such a corpus is itself a contribution we leave for future work.

\paragraph{Cross-Turkic transfer.} Although we situate Kyrgyz relative to Turkish and other Turkic languages, our experiments do not quantify cross-Turkic transfer (e.g.\ training on Turkish punctuation and evaluating on Kyrgyz). Given the sharp differences in script (Cyrillic vs.\ Latin) and available resources, we expect moderate transfer only, but a systematic study would strengthen the broader Turkic-NLP literature.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented the first punctuation restoration system for Kyrgyz, comparing three transformer models---mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa-base, and the Kyrgyz-specific monolingual KyrgyzBERT---fine-tuned on an augmented dataset of 16{,}028~sentences. XLM-RoBERTa-base achieves the best performance with a weighted F1-score of 0.944, outperforming mBERT~(0.930), KyrgyzBERT~(0.921), and a rule-based baseline~(0.768). Contrary to a simple \emph{specialization-beats-generalization} expectation, the Kyrgyz-specific KyrgyzBERT---although attractive from an inference-efficiency perspective ($7.8\times$ fewer parameters, up to $4.3\times$ faster inference)---does not surpass XLM-RoBERTa on this task; the pre-training scale advantage of the multilingual model dominates.

Per-class analysis confirms that period restoration is near-perfect (F1~0.97) across all models, while comma prediction remains the most challenging class (F1~0.767 for XLM-R) due to the interaction between Kyrgyz converb constructions and punctuation conventions. A targeted expansion of question-bearing sentences raises \texttt{QUESTION}-class F1 by 0.13 (XLM-R) to 0.33 (KyrgyzBERT), illustrating that the remaining gap on rare-class performance is primarily a data-imbalance problem. An ASR-integration experiment on Common~Voice Kyrgyz shows that even a Kyrgyz-fine-tuned Whisper produces zero commas across 200~utterances, while our post-processor brings sentence-terminal coverage to 100\% and adds~589 commas.

Future work will focus on: (1)~a Kyrgyz audio corpus with ground-truth punctuation to enable direct end-to-end evaluation of the ASR+post-processor pipeline, (2)~domain adaptation or confidence thresholding to mitigate comma over-insertion on short ASR utterances, (3)~cross-Turkic transfer learning using Turkish, Kazakh, and Uzbek data, (4)~incorporating morphological features to improve comma prediction at converb boundaries, (5)~extending coverage to paired and rare punctuation marks via structured decoders and focal loss, and (6)~integrating prosodic features for joint ASR--punctuation models.

\begin{quote}

\end{quote}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} The new Section~\ref{sec:punct-inventory} (``Punctuation Inventory of Written Kyrgyz'') lists every punctuation mark attested in our corpus~--- 12~marks in total~--- together with frequency, function, and a yes/no flag for model coverage (Table~\ref{tab:punct-inventory}). We then explicitly justify the exclusion of each unmodeled mark: \texttt{!} is almost never sentence-final in written Kyrgyz; the dash~(\textendash) is a copula substitute whose placement depends on the syntactic parse; \texttt{«»}, \texttt{""}, \texttt{()} are paired marks that token-level classification cannot balance; \texttt{:}, \texttt{;}, \texttt{\textemdash}, \texttt{\ldots} each constitute $<$1\% of all punctuation tokens. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} has been revised to cross-reference this inventory and name each excluded mark.
